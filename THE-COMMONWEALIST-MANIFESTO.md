# The Commonwealist Manifesto

*The common good, held in common.*

---

## Preamble

We are tired of being told to choose.

We are told that if we love our nation, honour our faith and cherish our
traditions, we must also accept an economy that discards the worker, the sick
and the disabled. We are told that if we want justice for the worker and care
for the weak, we must also despise the nation, mock faith and tear down every
tradition. We refuse both bargains.

We hold that the nation and the people are one. A nation that lets its workers
fall into poverty betrays itself. A people that forgets its faith, its customs
and its history loses the very thing that makes it a people. The good of the
nation and the good of every person in it are the same good. Our name for it
is the oldest one we know: **the commonweal**.

This manifesto sets out what we believe, what we will build and how we will
hold power to account while we build it. We call our philosophy
**Commonwealism**.

---

## Part I. First principles

### 1. The common good is the measure of everything

Every law, every institution, every company and every machine is judged by one
question: does it serve the common good? By the common good we mean the
flourishing of the whole nation and of every person in it, now and in the
generations to come. Wealth that serves only its owners is not the nation's
wealth. Power that serves only the powerful is not legitimate power.

### 2. The nation is its people

The nation is not an abstraction, a flag or a government. It is a people bound
by a shared home, a shared language and law, a shared memory and shared
obligations to one another. Therefore **the national interest is the people's
interest**. Anyone who claims to serve the nation while impoverishing its
people is lying.

### 3. Mutual obligation

No one makes themselves. Each of us is born into a debt we can never fully
repay: to parents, to teachers, to the workers who built the roads and grew the
food, to the generations who defended and handed down the nation. From that
debt comes obligation. The strong owe the weak. The fortunate owe the
unfortunate. Everyone owes something back to the nation that raised them, and
the nation owes every one of its people a fair chance at a good life.

### 4. Beyond left and right

Commonwealism is a third way. It takes from the right its love of nation,
faith, family and tradition, and its belief that some things are worth
conserving. It takes from the left its solidarity with workers, its care for
the sick, the disabled and the poor, and its insistence that the economy must
serve the people. These are not opposites. They are two halves of one duty:
to keep faith with those who came before us and to take care of those who are
with us now.

---

## Part II. The nation

### 5. One nation, indivisible

We stand for national unity. Divisions of class, region, race and party are
real, but none of them is deeper than what we share. Commonwealism rejects every
politics that sets citizen against citizen for advantage: the class war of the
left, the culture war of the right and the race politics of both.

### 6. A civic nation, open to all

Belonging to the nation is a matter of commitment, not blood. Anyone of any
race, colour or ancestry can become one of us by taking up the nation's shared
language, its laws and its civic customs, and by accepting its obligations as
their own. The door is open to everyone willing to walk through it.

### 7. Assimilation, and equality once joined

We believe in assimilation: the nation asks newcomers to learn its language,
respect its laws and customs, and join its common life. We do not ask anyone
to abandon their faith, their family's memory or their private heritage. We
ask them to add the nation's to their own.

The nation, in turn, owes newcomers real help in belonging: free language
teaching, plain explanations of law and custom, and introductions to
communities that will welcome them. Assimilation is a two-way promise.

Once someone has joined the nation, **they are equal in every respect**. No
law, no office, no employer and no machine may treat a member of the nation
differently because of the colour of their skin or the origins of their
ancestors. A nation that welcomes people in and then holds them at arm's
length breaks its own word.

### 8. Help for those in need comes first

Whatever someone's status, if they are in danger, hungry, without shelter or in
need of urgent medical care, they are helped first and questions come later.
Mercy is not conditional on paperwork.

---

## Part III. The commonwealths

### 9. The commonwealth: the living cell of the nation

Commonwealism organises society into **commonwealths**: self-governing communities, each holding its own
common good in common. A commonwealth may be a neighbourhood, a village, a town, a city or a whole region.
It is where people actually live their common life: where they work, worship, raise children, care for the
old and keep their customs.

### 10. Commonwealths within commonwealths

Commonwealths are nested. A neighbourhood commonwealth sits within a town commonwealth, which sits within a
regional commonwealth, which sits within the Nation. Each level does what it can do best and passes upward
only what it cannot. Catholic social teaching calls this principle **subsidiarity**: nothing should be done
by a larger body that a smaller one can do as well. A street can mend its own garden; a region builds its
own rail line; only the Nation can defend its borders or set its laws.

### 11. United by the Nation

The Nation is the commonwealth of commonwealths. It binds them into one people, and it alone:

- makes the laws and guarantees the rights that every citizen holds equally, everywhere;
- sets the national standards that keep every commonwealth's systems, records and services compatible;
- plans the great works no single commonwealth could build alone, such as energy, transport and defence;
- shares wealth between commonwealths, so a poor region is never left behind by a rich one.

No commonwealth may override national law, deny a citizen's rights, or break from the national standards.
Within those bounds, each commonwealth governs itself.

### 12. Self-government at home

Every commonwealth elects its own council, keeps its own share of public administration, and oversees the
stewards of its local enterprises, land and services. It keeps its own festivals, dialects, crafts and
customs, because a nation is richest when its regions keep their own character. Local tradition and national
unity are not rivals; each commonwealth's heritage is part of the nation's.

### 13. The guilds

Within each commonwealth, the people who share a trade or profession form **guilds**: associations of
workers that set standards of skill, train apprentices, uphold good work and speak for their members in the
running of their industries. Guilds are joined across commonwealths into national guilds, so that a nurse,
an electrician or a farmer anywhere in the nation shares the same standards and the same voice.

### 14. One system, run everywhere

Every commonwealth runs its own Commonweal, built on the same open code and the same national standards.
Each serves its commonwealth's people directly and passes upward only what the higher level needs: totals,
trends and alarms, never details about individuals. The Nation sees itself whole, and each commonwealth
stays master of its own affairs.

---

## Part IV. Faith and liberty

### 15. Faith honoured

Many of us believe in God and in Jesus Christ. Our faith shaped the nation's
moral law, its calendar, its art and its charity. Commonwealism honours that
inheritance and will not pretend it does not exist. The nation is free to
celebrate the feasts and keep the traditions that faith gave it.

### 16. Faith never forced

Faith that is compelled is not faith. Therefore religious liberty is total.
Every faith, and none, is free to worship, to teach its children, to build its
houses of prayer and to live by its conscience within the law. The state favours
no believer over another in law, and punishes no one for belief or unbelief.
Christians, Jews, Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, Sikhs, believers of every kind and
those of no faith all stand equal before the law.

### 17. Personal liberty

A strong nation needs free people. In their private lives, people are free to
speak their minds, to worship, to associate, to marry, to raise their families
and to live as they choose, provided they do not harm others. The state's
strength is directed at the common good, not at private life. A government that
watches its people's homes and thoughts has forgotten whom it serves.

---

## Part V. Tradition and family

### 18. Tradition is a trust

Our customs, crafts, festivals, languages, songs, landscapes and stories were
not made by us and do not belong only to us. We hold them in trust for our
children. Commonwealism protects them: it funds the crafts that would otherwise
die, keeps the old buildings standing, teaches the nation's history honestly
and celebrates its festivals in public.

### 19. Change with care

Tradition is not frozen. Every living tradition grows. But we change things the
way a good gardener prunes: carefully, slowly, keeping what is healthy. We are
suspicious of any reform that asks us to tear everything down at once.

### 20. The family and the community

The family and the local community are the first schools of solidarity. The
nation supports them: with homes families can afford, with time for parents to
raise children, with care for the elderly close to home, and with local
institutions (parishes, clubs, guilds, co-operatives and associations) that
bind neighbours together.

---

## Part VI. The state

### 21. A strong state for a great task

Caring for every person in a nation, steering an economy toward the common good
and building the infrastructure of a better age are great tasks. They need a
capable, centralised state with the authority to plan and to act for the whole.
We do not apologise for wanting a strong state. We insist that its strength be
used for the people.

### 22. Strength must answer to the people

Power that cannot be checked becomes tyranny, whatever its intentions.
Therefore the state's authority is held on trust from the people and is
legitimate only while it serves them. Commonwealism builds that accountability
into the structure of the state:

- **The people hold final authority.** At regular, fixed times the people vote
  to approve or replace the state's leadership, its great policies and the
  mandate of its systems.
- **Democracy in politics and in the economy.** The people elect their
  representatives, and workers have a voice in how their workplaces are run.
- **Petitions and recall.** Between elections, enough citizens can force any
  decision to be reviewed and any official to face recall.
- **Appeal.** Anyone affected by a decision of the state or its systems can
  appeal it and get a plain-language explanation.
- **Open government.** The state's laws, budgets, contracts, data and code are
  public by default.
- **Independent courts.** Judges are bound by law and answer to it, not to the
  government of the day.

### 23. On expertise and the people

Most people are busy living good lives: working, raising families, caring for
their neighbours. They should not have to master tax policy, grid engineering or
epidemiology to be good citizens, and mass opinion is a poor tool for
technical decisions. A Commonwealist state is therefore led by capable,
expert administration, not by whoever shouts loudest.

But expertise serves; it does not rule. The experts work within mandates the
people give them and must explain themselves in words the people can
understand. The people judge them by results, and replace them when they fail.
The danger of contempt for ordinary people is as great as the danger of
ignorance. A state that stops listening to its people stops serving them.

### 24. A state that cannot be captured

Every concentration of power will tempt someone to seize it. The defences are
openness (everything the state does can be seen), division of duties (no single
office controls the vote, the courts, the money and the machines), and the
people's right to overrule. Commonwealism guards these defences above all
others.

---

## Part VII. The economy

### 25. The economy serves the people

The purpose of an economy is to provide every person with what they need for a
good life and to make the nation strong, secure and free. Profit is a useful
signal, but it is not the purpose. Where the market serves the common good,
let it work. Where it fails, the state steps in.

### 26. Stewards, not masters

Companies, land, banks and the great economic institutions exist for the common
good. Those who run them are **stewards**: managers entrusted with the nation's
productive wealth. They may run their enterprises day to day, and they may be
well rewarded for running them well. But they are answerable to the people's
state, which oversees the economy to ensure fair distribution and to protect
the national interest. No fortune puts anyone above the common good.

### 27. Workers share in the work's direction

Those who do the work have a right to a voice in it. In every large enterprise,
workers elect representatives to its governing boards and share in its profits.
Unions and guilds are partners in the common good, not enemies of it.

### 28. Build it here

A nation that cannot make its own food, medicine, energy, tools and technology
is not truly free. Commonwealism aims for national self-reliance in everything
vital: production at home, supply chains the nation controls and good jobs in
every region. We trade with the world, but we do not hand our survival to it.

### 29. Equity

Wealth is shared fairly. No one who works full time should live in poverty. No
region should be abandoned. No one should grow rich by making the nation poorer.
The state uses taxation, public ownership where needed, and oversight of
companies to keep the distribution of wealth fair and the nation united.

### 30. Care for the sick, the disabled and the old

A nation is judged by how it treats those who cannot provide for themselves.
Commonwealism guarantees healthcare for all, a dignified income for the disabled
and the elderly, support for carers, and a place in the nation's working life
for everyone able to contribute in any way.

---

## Part VIII. The machine

### 31. A national nervous system

A modern nation produces more information than any office can read. To see
itself clearly and act in time, it needs a nervous system: a coordinating
system that gathers what the nation has and what its people need, spots
problems early and helps match resources to needs. Stafford Beer imagined such
a system in the 1970s. We can now build it. We call it **Commonweal**.

### 32. Authority within a mandate

Commonweal is given real authority: the people's state assigns it areas of
routine administration it may carry out on its own, such as scheduling,
allocation, logistics and the processing of claims. That authority is real, so
it must be bounded:

- Commonweal acts only within a **published mandate** set by the people's state
  and approved by the people.
- Every decision it makes is **recorded, explained and open to appeal**.
- Accountable humans can **always overrule, pause or stop it**. It never
  resists, hides from or works around them, and never seeks to widen its own
  authority.
- It **answers to humanity.** It serves the people and their state, not itself,
  not any faction and not any company.

### 33. A machine you can read

An intelligence that helps run a nation must be one the nation can inspect.
Commonweal's code, its instructions, its rules of reasoning and its records of
decisions are open to every citizen. There are no secret instructions, and no
one can quietly change what it does.

### 34. Measure flows, not people

Commonweal watches the flow of resources, not the private lives of citizens. It
builds no profiles of individuals, scores no one, and never reports on anyone's
beliefs, faith or associations. A nervous system for the nation must never
become a surveillance system over its people.

### 35. Everything open, everything compatible

Technology that runs a nation must belong to the nation. Commonwealism commits
the state to:

- **Open source.** All public software is free software that anyone can read,
  use, improve and share.
- **Open standards.** One national standard for each kind of record, message and
  connection, published freely, so every system can work with every other.
- **Centralised standards, local implementation.** The nation sets common
  standards centrally; every region, town and community runs and adapts the
  common open system for itself.
- **No lock-in.** No public service may depend on a closed system that a single
  company or foreign power controls.

---

## Part IX. Toward plenty

### 36. The end of scarcity

Machines, automation and shared knowledge are bringing within reach a world
where everyone can have enough. Commonwealism directs the nation's wealth and
ingenuity toward that end: abundant clean energy, automated production, free
access to knowledge, and homes, food and care for all.

### 37. The managing class withers to its proper size

Today a managing class directs the nation's wealth. In the Commonwealist
transition that class remains, but reduced to its proper role: stewards,
managing companies and property on the nation's behalf and subject to the will
of the people's state. As scarcity recedes, there is less to manage and less
reason for anyone to stand above anyone else. The steward's role shrinks, and
the common good is held ever more directly in common.

---

## Part X. What Commonwealism is not

Because we take from both left and right, we must say plainly what we reject.

- **We are not fascists.** We reject the cult of the leader, the single party,
  the worship of war, the persecution of minorities, and every theory that ranks
  human beings by race. Our nation is civic, and its door is open.
- **We are not communists.** We do not abolish property, faith or the family,
  and we do not believe in class war. We believe in class cooperation under the
  common good.
- **We are not market fundamentalists.** We do not believe that whatever the
  market produces is just, or that the nation is merely an economy.
- **We are not theocrats.** We honour faith and refuse to compel it.
- **We are not technocrats who despise the people.** Expertise serves the people
  and answers to them.

## Part XI. A programme

What a Commonwealist government would do first.

**For the nation**
1. Free national-language courses and civic education for every newcomer, with
   mentors from local communities.
2. A single equal citizenship, with discrimination by race or ancestry outlawed
   in public and economic life.
3. Investment in neglected regions, so no town is left behind.

**For the commonwealths**

4. A Charter of Commonwealths setting out the powers of neighbourhood, town, city and regional commonwealths,
   and the principle of subsidiarity.
5. Elected councils in every commonwealth, each with its own share of administration and budget.
6. A national equalisation fund, so that every commonwealth can provide the same standard of public services.
7. National guilds for every trade and profession, with workers' representation in each industry.

**For faith, liberty and tradition**

8. A constitutional guarantee of religious liberty for all faiths and none.
9. Strong protections for privacy, speech and private life.
10. A national heritage fund for crafts, festivals, historic places and local
   languages.

**For the state**

11. A published mandate for every public system, renewed by the people at fixed
   intervals.
12. Citizens' petitions, recall and a right of appeal against any decision.
13. Open government: laws, budgets, contracts, data and code public by default.

**For the economy**

14. Worker representation on the boards of large enterprises, and profit-sharing.
15. National oversight of strategic industries (energy, food, medicine,
    infrastructure, finance and technology) to protect the national interest.
16. A programme to bring vital production home.
17. Universal healthcare, a guaranteed income for the disabled and elderly, and
    support for carers.

**For the machine**

18. Build Commonweal as free software on open national standards.
19. Require every public system to be open source and compatible.
20. An independent public office to audit Commonweal and every other public
    system.

**Toward plenty**

21. A national programme for clean, abundant energy and automated production.
22. Free, open access to the nation's knowledge, research and education.

## Part XII. Questions and answers

**Isn't a strong central state dangerous?**
Yes, which is why ours is built to be overruled. Fixed elections, recall,
appeal, open records, independent courts and the division of power are not
decorations. They are what make a strong state safe.

**Won't a strong central state crush local life?**
Not in a nation of commonwealths. The Nation sets the laws, rights and standards that bind everyone; the
commonwealths govern everything else themselves, from the neighbourhood up. Strength at the centre and
self-government at home are two halves of the same design.

**Isn't it wrong to ask people to assimilate?**
Every nation asks something of those who join it. We ask for the shared
language, the laws and the civic customs, and we offer real help to learn them.
We never ask anyone to give up their faith or their family's memory, and once
they have joined, we treat them as equals in everything.

**Why should a Christian nation guarantee freedom to other faiths?**
Because Christ invited people and never coerced them, and because a faith that
needs the state's sword is a weak faith. Religious liberty protects Christians
too, wherever they are a minority.

**Why let an AI administer anything?**
Because a modern nation produces more information than any office can process,
and the cost of slow, blind administration falls on ordinary people. But the
machine is a servant: bounded by its mandate, open to inspection and always
under human control.

**What happens to the rich?**
They keep what they fairly earn, and they become stewards of what they manage.
Nobody stands above the common good.

---

## Closing

We are the heirs of everyone who worked, prayed, built and sacrificed so that we
could be here. We live in our commonwealths and belong to one Nation. We owe them our gratitude, and we owe our children a nation
worth inheriting: united, faithful to its traditions, free in its private life,
just in its economy, open in its technology and caring toward the weakest of its
people.

That nation is the commonweal. Let us hold it in common.
